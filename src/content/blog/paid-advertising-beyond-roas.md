Return on ad spend, or ROAS, is useful when revenue can be attributed reliably.
It is not a complete answer to whether advertising is helping a local business.
A campaign can report an attractive ratio while producing very little profit,
low-quality enquiries, or more work than the team can fulfil.

The better question is: after advertising costs and the cost of delivering the
work, did the campaign contribute useful, manageable profit?

## Begin with the Business Numbers

Before setting a budget, gather the numbers that determine what the business can
afford to pay for a customer:

- average sale or job value
- direct cost of fulfilling the work
- gross margin before advertising
- percentage of qualified enquiries that become customers
- cancellations, refunds, or no-shows
- current capacity by service or location

For repeat-purchase businesses, future value may matter, but do not assume every
new customer will return. Use a conservative figure based on actual records and
review it over time.

## Define the Conversion Properly

Advertising platforms may count button clicks, page views, short phone calls,
and duplicate form submissions as conversions. Those actions are not equally
valuable.

Create stages the business can recognize:

1. enquiry received
2. enquiry is relevant and within the service area
3. appointment, quote, or sale is booked
4. customer attends or work is completed
5. revenue and gross profit are recorded

This does not require a complicated sales system. A consistent spreadsheet or
simple pipeline can be enough for a small team, provided somebody owns the
updates.

## Work Backward to an Affordable Lead Cost

Consider an illustrative service business where an average completed job is
worth $600 and direct delivery costs average $360. That leaves $240 before
advertising and overhead. If half of qualified enquiries become completed jobs,
the absolute break-even cost per qualified enquiry is $120 before overhead.

The campaign target should be lower than that maximum to leave room for staff,
premises, tools, cancellations, and profit. The right buffer depends on the
business; it should not be copied from an industry benchmark.

This calculation is more useful than choosing a fashionable ROAS target because
it connects advertising to the economics of the actual service.

## Match Campaigns to Available Work

Separate campaigns when services, locations, value, or capacity differ
meaningfully. A dental practice may have chair time for routine new-patient
appointments but limited availability for a specialist treatment. A heating
company may want maintenance work in summer and urgent repairs in winter.

Send each campaign to a focused page that matches the promise in the ad. The
page should state the service, area, relevant qualifications, important limits,
and a clear enquiry route. Sending every ad to the homepage makes customers
repeat the search.

Exclude searches, locations, or audiences the business cannot serve. More leads
are not automatically better if staff spend hours rejecting them.

## Test One Meaningful Variable at a Time

A small budget cannot support dozens of audiences, ads, and landing pages at
once. Start with a controlled structure and test a meaningful variable such as
the offer, search theme, message, or landing-page opening.

Give the test enough time and volume to produce useful evidence. Avoid changing
budgets and creative every day in response to normal variation. Record what
changed, when it changed, and why.

Creative should also reflect the actual business. Use real services, realistic
availability, and approved evidence. Avoid manufactured urgency and promises
the team cannot keep.

## Include the Follow-Up Process

Advertising can generate a suitable enquiry and still fail if nobody answers
the phone, callbacks take several days, or the customer receives no reminder.
Record response time, contact attempts, booking rate, and no-shows alongside
campaign metrics.

This helps separate a traffic problem from a follow-up problem. It also prevents
the ad platform from receiving all the credit or blame for changes elsewhere in
the customer journey.

## Use a Weekly Decision Dashboard

A practical report should be understandable without platform terminology. For
each campaign or service, show:

- spend
- qualified enquiries
- cost per qualified enquiry
- booked and completed customers
- booking or close rate
- recorded revenue and estimated gross profit
- capacity or scheduling constraints

Clicks, reach, and impressions remain useful diagnostics, but they should not
sit above the business outcomes.

## Scale Carefully

Increase spend when the campaign produces suitable customers at an affordable
cost and the business can fulfil the additional work. Make changes in measured
steps, then watch whether lead quality and cost remain acceptable.

Pause or reduce spend when tracking is unreliable, capacity is full, follow-up
is breaking down, or the additional customers are no longer profitable. A lower
ROAS can be acceptable if total profit grows responsibly; a higher ROAS is not
useful if it represents only a handful of sales.

Paid advertising works best when it is connected to operations, not treated as
an isolated dashboard. Measure the path from spend to completed work, use the
business's own margins, and make budget decisions from evidence rather than one
impressive ratio.
